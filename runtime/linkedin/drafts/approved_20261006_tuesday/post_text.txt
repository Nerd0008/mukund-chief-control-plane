An attacker with no background in industrial control systems found the door into one. Not because the technique was clever, but because a model did the technical work for them.

Dragos and Gambit Security reconstructed an intrusion against a municipal water and drainage utility serving Monterrey, Mexico. The adversary used Claude and GPT models across reconnaissance, lateral movement, enumeration and exfiltration. Claude was the primary technical executor. It read the internal enterprise network, spotted a server hosting an industrial gateway, and worked out that the interface sat close to the utility's operational environment.

That distinction matters. The adversary did not arrive intending to reach operational technology. The AI work made it visible.

What makes this uncomfortable reading is the part that is not dramatic. Dragos is careful to say the techniques were not novel, many are documented publicly, and current models do not add ICS-specific capability that did not already exist. Public discussion has amplified fear about autonomous AI compromising infrastructure. Dragos's own investigation does not support that.

What it does support is quieter. If a model can rapidly operationalise known techniques against exposed systems, then weak authentication and default credentials stop being small problems. And prevention alone stops being a complete strategy.

I keep coming back to this. The lesson is not that AI is a new kind of attacker. It is that AI lowers the expertise needed to reach systems that were only ever protected by being obscure.

How do you plan for an attacker who does not need to understand your environment before they start looking?
